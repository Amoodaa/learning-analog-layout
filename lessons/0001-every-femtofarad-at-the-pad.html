Lesson 0001 · SerDes RX front-end layout

Every femtofarad at the pad

How the RX input node sets a bandwidth ceiling, and why your layout decides where it is.

Your win for today Given a capacitance budget for the RX pad node, compute the input pole and its loss at Nyquist, and name the layout features that feed each term. About 15 minutes.

The chain you'll be drawing

A wireline receiver front end is a short chain. The signal arrives at the bump and pad, meets the ESD clamps, meets the on-die termination, and enters the CTLE (continuous-time linear equalizer). The CTLE and the gain stages after it shape the signal for the sampler.The AFE "provides equalization (CTLE) and gain stages (VGA) to optimize the signal for symbol detection." Palermo, ECEN 720 L6, slide 11. Modern 56–112 Gb/s receivers also put a T-coil between the pad and the rest, a topic for a later lesson.Palermo L6, slides 8–9, shows the input networks of two ISSCC 2019 PAM4 receivers. In both, a T-coil "isolates ESD and input stage capacitance."

The node that sets the ceiling

Links are built to 50 Ω so the signal doesn't reflect."Signal integrity considerations (min. reflections) requires 50 Ω" driver impedance. Palermo, ECEN 620 High-Speed I/O, slide 19. At the RX pad, the incoming line looks like a 50 Ω source, and the on-die termination is another 50 Ω to the common-mode voltage. Since they're in parallel, the node sees only 25 Ω, and every capacitor in the figure loads it. That makes a single pole:

Reff = Z0 ∥ RT = 25 Ω     fp = 1 / (2π · Reff · Ctotal)

Compare that pole with the Nyquist frequency, half the symbol rate. That's where channel loss is quoted and where the eye is hurt most."It is customary to specify the channel loss at the 'Nyquist frequency,' fNyq, i.e., the frequency equal to half of the data rate." Razavi, "The Design of an Equalizer, Part One", SSC Magazine 2021. PAM4 sends 2 bits per symbol, so 112 Gb/s PAM4 runs at 56 GBd and has the same 28 GHz Nyquist as 56 Gb/s NRZ.

Worked example (illustrative numbers)

Say the node carries 350 fF in total. Then fp = 1 / (2π · 25 Ω · 350 fF) ≈ 18.2 GHz. At 56 Gb/s NRZ (Nyquist 28 GHz), one pole costs 20·log10(1/√(1 + (28/18.2)²)) ≈ −5.3 dB before the channel has done anything. The CTLE has to buy that back, and it pays in noise and power. Adding another 10 fF at this point lowers the pole by about 0.5 GHz.These capacitance values are made up to give a sense of scale. Compare them privately against your own PDK and extraction. Don't bring work numbers into this workspace.

Where the femtofarads come from: your layout

TermLayout features that set itWho usually owns it
CpadPad and bump metal area over substrate, metal under the pad, pad shieldingI/O / package + layout
CESDDiode junction area and perimeter, set by the ESD current they must surviveESD engineer + layout
CRTResistor body over substrate or well, its contacts and wiring to the nodeLayout
CrouteWidth, length, layer choice and neighbours of the pad → CTLE wiring; via stacksLayout
CinCTLE input-pair gates (designer sizes them); gate/MOL wiring and fingers (you)Designer + layout

Here is the main tension in this job. ESD wants big diodes and wide metal, because the discharge current has to flow somewhere without melting it. Bandwidth wants the smallest possible capacitance on this exact node. Galal & Razavi's answer was to hide the ESD capacitance behind a T-coil, which recovered 20 dB of return loss at 10 GHz in 0.18 µm.Galal & Razavi, JSSC Dec 2003. T-coils get their own lesson later. Before you reach for that, though, every fF of routing and resistor parasitics you avoid is bandwidth you get for free.

Play with the budget

Try these in order. Predict before you drag.

  1. With the defaults at 56 Gb/s NRZ, which term is the biggest lever? Halve it and see what the pole does.
  2. Switch to 112 Gb/s PAM4. Why does the Nyquist frequency not move?
  3. Find a budget that puts fp at or above 28 GHz. What's the total C? (Hold onto that number for the quiz.)

Retrieval practice

Don't scroll up. Forcing the recall is the part that makes it stick.

1. A 50 Ω line drives an RX pad with 50 Ω on-die termination. What single-ended resistance does the pad-node capacitance see?

2. Same 25 Ω node, total capacitance 400 fF. Where is the input pole?

3. What is the Nyquist frequency of a 112 Gb/s PAM4 link?

4. To keep the input pole at or above 28 GHz on a 25 Ω node, what is the maximum total capacitance?

At work tomorrow

Real-world step (≈ 20 min, stays at work) Pick any pad-connected net you've laid out, even a slow GPIO. Run your usual extraction (StarRC in a Custom Compiler flow) and list its total capacitance by contributor: pad, device, routing per layer. Rank the top three, and ask yourself which of them you could have changed. Keep the numbers at work. Bring back only the ranking and anything that surprised you.
